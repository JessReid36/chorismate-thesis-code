Why there are two layers. The substrate carries a charge of −2, so a sphere's charge decides how close it can sit.

  • A +1 sphere is pulled in. In Stage 2's free tests, +1 spheres came to rest closer than plain touching distance, about 2.75 Å from O3. The cation layer sits at those measured closest distances, and only positive spheres may use it.
  • A −1 sphere is pushed away. In Stage 2 none stayed anywhere, so it can get no closer than touching distance (Lennard-Jones contact). The anion/neutral layer sits there, and any charge may use it, because farther out is always allowed. That is why +1 spheres also appear on it.
  • Beside the OH hydrogen (H8) it is the other way round. H8 is slightly positive, so a −1 sphere may come in to 2.33 Å, while a +1 sphere stays about 3.7 Å away. There the cation-layer sites lie outside the anion/neutral layer. Since Stage 5 Amendment 2 they admit +1 only, so negative and neutral spheres sit at touching distance everywhere, H8 included.

One layer would have put either charge in the wrong place: positive spheres held farther out than they really come, or negative spheres closer than any ever gets. A design built on impossible contacts would fall apart when the molecule relaxes.

What is plotted. Only the sites that admit the chosen charge are shown, from the amended development grid cs_stage5/exploratory/dev_sites_a2.tsv (Stage 5 Amendment 2):

  • +1 e: 2552 sites. The 217 anion/neutral-layer sites beside H8 admit no positive charge.
  • −1 e: 1541 sites, all on the anion/neutral layer (Stage 5 Amendment 2: charges ≤ 0 only there).

Both overlays the two: +1 e spheres as circles, −1 e as diamonds, each ranked within its own charge. Sites on the anion/neutral layer admit both charges. Because the two effects are equal and opposite, such a site appears in one row for +1 and in the other row for −1.

Values come from dev_A/A_dev.tsv (results 6edfbb1). Each is the first-order electrostatic change at frozen geometry, so a −1 sphere has exactly the opposite effect of a +1 sphere at the same place. The steric term L and polarisation are not included.

Negative spheres. No negative sphere found a resting place in Stage 2. The −1 positions are therefore Lennard-Jones contact, or 2.331 Å at H8, and a sphere would have to be held there. Net-negative arrangements also push the dianion's highest occupied orbital up (Stage 4c).

  • Colour. Each residue's carbons and sticks are coloured by its rank in the literature for catalytic importance, red (1, most) to green (6, least), on the page's ramp. The rank is also shown in each label. It follows Szefczyk et al. (2004, Table 1), who order the residues by MP2 differential transition-state stabilisation: Arg90 −9.06, Arg7 −5.90, Glu78 −3.57, Arg116 −2.45, Arg63 −1.40, Lys60 +1.39 kcal/mol (Lys60 raises the barrier). Claeyssens et al. (2011, Table 3; 16 B3LYP QM/MM pathways) give the same order for the five they report (Arg90 6.0, Arg7 3.1, Glu78 2.1, Arg116 1.0, Arg63 −0.6 kcal/mol of transition-state stabilisation).
    • Glu78's contribution depends strongly on the hydroxyl's orientation and distance: −8.70 to +7.33 kcal/mol in Szefczyk's Tables 2–3.
    • Arg63's sign changes between theory levels in Szefczyk's Table 1, and on average it slightly destabilises the transition state in Claeyssens'.
    • At the simplest electrostatic levels Arg7 edges out Arg90.
    • This is the literature's ranking, not this project's: Stage 4d at frame 41786 differs in sign for Arg7 and Arg116.
  • What they are not. These are the enzyme's own residues, shown for orientation. They are not grid sites.
